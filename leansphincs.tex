\section{The scheme}

Same shape as SLH-DSA-128-24 from NIST's recent SP~800-230~\cite{SP800230} (single XMSS layer), with the differences:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $h = 26$, instead of $22$ (tree height, i.e. height of the only XMSS layer)
    \item $k = 24$, instead of $6$ (number of trees per FORS)
    \item $a = 10$, instead of $24$ (height of each FORS tree)
    \item WOTS$^+$C~\cite{HK22C}, instead of WOTS. 64 chains of length $w=4$, with a target sum of 120
  \end{itemize}

Properties:

\begin{itemize}
  \item public key: 32 bytes
  \item secret key: 32 bytes
  \item signature size: 5\,684 bytes
  \item verification: 391 compressions (with BLAKE2s)
  \item security: NIST level 1 \cite{FIPS205} (applies to all the usages below) (127 bits SUF in the ROM, and $\approx 64$ bits in the QROM)
  \item lifetime: up to $2^{30}$ signatures.
\end{itemize}

\section{Usage}

\subsection{Full \texorpdfstring{$2^{30}$}{2\textasciicircum 30} lifetime}

For powerful devices.

\begin{itemize}
  \item lifetime: $2^{30}$
  \item keygen: $19 \cdot 10^9$ compressions
  \item signing 
    \begin{itemize}
    \item with a 1~MiB cache: $421\cdot10^3$ compressions
    \item with a 1~GiB cache: $75\cdot10^3$ compressions
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\emph{Modern laptop CPUs can compute on the order of $10^9$ SHA2 or BLAKE2s compressions per second.}


\subsection{Pruned: \texorpdfstring{$2^{17}$}{2\textasciicircum 17} lifetime}\label{sec:pruned}

For weaker devices. The trick is to trade smaller lifetime for faster keygen, using hypertree pruning~\cite{conduition}: only build one subtree of height 13, and replace the 13 siblings above it by pseudorandom surrogate nodes.

\begin{itemize}
  \item lifetime: $2^{17}$
  \item keygen: $2.2\cdot10^6$ compressions
  \item signing: $111\cdot10^3$ compressions, with a 16~KiB cache (including $33\cdot10^3$ compressions of grinding)
\end{itemize}

\emph{A Ledger Nano S+ can compute $33$ (resp $25$) thousand BLAKE2s (resp SHA2) per second.}

\subsection{Pruned: \texorpdfstring{$2^{24}$}{2\textasciicircum 24} lifetime}

For laptops, phones and powerful hardware wallets. The same pruning, with a subtree of height 20 (and 6
surrogate siblings).

\begin{itemize}
  \item lifetime: $2^{24}$
  \item keygen: $287\cdot10^6$ compressions
  \item signing: $84\cdot10^3$ compressions, with a 1~MiB cache (including 256 compressions of grinding)
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Threshold signing}

\subsubsection{3-of-4 with a DKG}

A concrete example of what's possible:
\begin{itemize}
  \item 3-of-4: any 3 operators can sign
  \item lifetime: $2^{16}$ signatures, with the hypertree pruning of \cref{sec:pruned} and a subtree of
        height 12
  \item malicious security against 1 operator, with abort (but 2 colluders can rebuild the key, unlike
        3-of-4 BLS)
  \item DKG: no trusted dealer
  \item keygen: $786\cdot10^3$ MPC hashes, 1.33~GB of traffic per operator and 2\,734 rounds (measured
        with the prototype, BLAKE2s)
  \item signing: thanks to caching of the sub-tree in XMSS, only one FORS has to be computed in MPC, $24\,696$ MPC hashes, 42~MB of
        traffic per operator and 1\,800 rounds. Either:
    \begin{itemize}
    \item vanilla, once the message is known (signing takes a few seconds)
    \item or with preprocessing: compute the instance before the message is known. Online, no MPC: only
          $\approx 2.0\cdot10^8$ compressions of grinding in the clear and 10 rounds of 7~KB in total
          per operator
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Idea.} Only hashes on secret inputs need MPC: WOTS chain steps and FORS leaves. The rest (trees,
grinding, WOTS$^+$C counter) runs in the clear. These hashes don't depend on the message, so a FORS
instance can be computed in advance, and the randomizer ground until the message selects it.

\paragraph{Secrets.} Each secret is the XOR of 4 terms, term $i$ known to every operator except
$i$~\cite{CDI05}: 1 operator learns nothing, any 2 know everything. Term $i$ is $\hash(k_i,\mathit{addr})$
for a seed $k_i$, so nothing is stored.

\paragraph{Keygen (DKG).} Each $k_i$ is generated jointly by its 3 holders (commit-reveal), so no holder can
relate it to another seed. The public parameter and the 14 surrogate nodes come from commit-reveal coin tossing
(a chosen surrogate could hide a known subtree). MPC hashes the 64 chains of the $2^{12}$ kept leaves to
their ends ($786\cdot10^3$ hashes) and opens the ends; the tree is built in the clear.

\paragraph{Signing.} The operators agree on $m$ and on a fresh joint nonce $s$ (commit-reveal), kept until the
signature is finished. They open $R_0=\bigoplus_i\hash(k_i,\mathsf{msg},m,s)$ (local hashes) and grind
$R=\hash(R_0,\mathit{ctr})$, taking the first counter that works:
\begin{itemize}
  \item vanilla: until the leaf index lands in the kept subtree, then compute the selected FORS instance
        in MPC and open the 24 selected secrets
  \item with preprocessing: until it lands on the next instance, drawn and computed in advance as
        $\bigoplus_i\hash(k_i,\mathsf{next},D)$, where $D\leftarrow\hash(D,R)$ after each finished signature (so
        $D$ never repeats), then open the 24 selected secrets
\end{itemize}
Instances stay random, so security is as for a plain pruned key. Computing an instance: MPC hashes its
$24\,576$ secrets into leaves, which are opened; the operators build the FORS root, run the WOTS$^+$C
counter search, compare hashes of the result, and MPC walks the WOTS chains to the signed positions (120
hashes).

\paragraph{Cheaters.} The MPC is maliciously secure with abort~\cite{BGIN19} and verified before any
opening; opened values are checked against public data or against the other holders of the same term. A
cheater can only cause aborts (identifying it would cost more), so the operators retry with each set of 3.
Once $R_0$ is opened, the signature is always finished: nobody can
preview FORS indices and pick which get opened.

\paragraph{MPC cost.} An MPC hash is a BLAKE2s compression on a secret input: about $14.7\cdot10^3$ AND
gates (XOR is free) and AND-depth 884, i.e.\ 884 rounds (2\,500 rounds is about 2 minutes at 50~ms per
round). Traffic per AND gate per operator, with malicious security:
\begin{itemize}
  \item about 1 bit: 3 operators~\cite{BGIN19} (the numbers above)
  \item about 1.5 bits: 4 operators~\cite{FF21,BS26}
  \item about 10 to 16 bits: 3 operators with cut-and-choose~\cite{FLNW17}
  \item about 8 to 16 bits: bigger clusters (e.g.\ 10-of-15), Shamir over $\mathrm{GF}(2^4)$ (our estimate
        from~\cite{ATLAS21})
\end{itemize}
With $n$ operators and $f<n/2$ corrupt (one seed per set of $f$ operators), $2f+1$ must be online; signing
despite $f$ cheaters needs $n>3f$.

\paragraph{Against 2 of 4.} One seed per pair (6 seeds) gives secrecy against 2, but 3 signers then have
no honest majority (and 2 cheaters can block signing), so the MPC must tolerate a dishonest majority:
\begin{itemize}
  \item authenticated garbling~\cite{YWZ20}: about 4\,100 bits per AND gate per operator, so 5.9~TB at
        keygen
  \item Beaver triples from a pseudorandom correlation generator, authenticated via PIR~\cite{BGHIN26}:
        about 4 bits per AND gate per operator (6~GB at keygen, 180~MB per FORS instance), but 15 to 150
        core-hours of computation per operator at keygen, and relies on recent LPN variants
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Key rotation.} The shares above cannot be rotated: new seeds would give new secrets, and
the public key commits to them. Rotation becomes possible if, as in SLH-DSA, every secret is
$\hash(S,\mathit{addr})$ for one master secret $S$, computed in MPC, and the operators share only $S$:
$S=t_1\oplus t_2\oplus t_3\oplus t_4$, with term $t_i$ held by every operator except $i$. A rotation needs
no dealer:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item every operator sends a random seed to the others
  \item from all the seeds, everyone expands the same 4 masks $z_1,\dots,z_4$ with $z_1\oplus z_2\oplus
        z_3\oplus z_4=0$, and every holder of $t_i$ replaces it by $t_i\oplus z_i$
  \item the holders of each term compare hashes of the new term, then delete the seeds and the old terms
\end{enumerate}
$S$ never changes and nobody learns it, but terms stolen before the rotation can't be combined with terms
stolen after (if at most 1 operator is corrupted in each period). To replace an operator, the 2 other
holders of each of its 3 terms send it to the newcomer, who checks that the copies match; a rotation then
makes the leaving operator's terms useless. The same idea also changes $n$ and the threshold, with the same public key: each
old term is split into fresh pieces for the new committee's terms (checked by its other holders), and the
old terms are deleted. The cost is one more MPC hash per secret: keygen
$1.05\cdot10^6$ MPC hashes (1.9~GB, 3\,300 rounds), and $49\cdot10^3$ per FORS instance (91~MB, 4\,200
rounds), about $1.3\times$ and $2\times$ today. The randomizer keeps its own seeds, so online signing stays
MPC-free.

\subsubsection{Any \texorpdfstring{$t$-of-$n$}{t-of-n} with a trusted dealer}

With a trusted dealer, signing needs no MPC at all, for any $t$-of-$n$, following PRAWNS~\cite{PRAWNS}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item lifetime: $2^{16}$ signatures (16 times more lifetime costs 16 times more public data and dealer
        time)
  \item keygen, by the dealer in the clear: $3\cdot10^8$ compressions, for all the FORS instances of the
        $2^{12}$ kept leaves
  \item public data: 1.6~GB (the FORS leaves, and 4~MB of WOTS signatures)
  \item signing: no MPC. Each signer sends 24 evaluation shares, and grinding plus the FORS paths take
        $74\cdot10^3$ compressions
\end{itemize}
How it works:
\begin{itemize}
  \item keygen: the dealer samples the key of a context-aware threshold PRF $F$ and sets every FORS secret of the $2^{12}$
        kept instances to $F(\mathit{addr})$. It builds the pruned key, publishes all the FORS leaves and the WOTS signature of every FORS root, gives each operator its share of $F$, and
        deletes everything else
  \item signing: the $t$ signers pick the randomizer by commit-reveal and grind it into the kept subtree.
        Each sends its evaluation shares of $F$ at the 24 opened FORS secrets, with the message as context
        (shares for different messages can't be combined, otherwise aborted attempts would leak FORS
        secrets). Anyone combines $t$ shares and checks each secret against its public leaf
\end{itemize}
This is secure against $t-1$ malicious signers, and $n$, $t$ and the shares
can change without changing the public key. For large $n$ and $t$ (say 30-of-40), $F$ is a lattice (LWE)
threshold PRF over Shamir shares, used only by the signers; for small $\binom{n}{t}$ it can be hash-based:
our seeds, plus pairwise masks that cancel only within one signing session. The price is trusting the dealer at keygen: PRAWNS has no efficient DKG.
