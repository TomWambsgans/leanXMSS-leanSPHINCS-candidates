\section{The scheme}
\label{sec:scheme}

A SPHINCS-style scheme with four toggles, 60 configurations in all. Its components:
\begin{itemize}
  \item few-time signature (FTS, T4): a \emph{two-level WOTS forest} (\cref{sec:twolevel}, after
        sig.golf~\cite{siggolf}), or FORS
  \item one-time signature: WOTS$^+$C~\cite{HK22C}, 64 chains of length $w=4$, target sum 128
  \item the FTS public key is the nodes just below the roots, two per tree (16 digests for the forest of 8
        trees, 48 for FORS of 24 trees); WOTS$^+$C signs them directly
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The toggles.}
\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item[T1, lifetime.] About $2^{24}$, $2^{26}$, $2^{28}$, $2^{30}$ or $2^{32}$ signatures. A pruned key has an index of
        $h=20$, 22, 24, 26 or 28 bits, and its full key lasts $N\approx19$--$21\cdot2^h$ signatures (FORS:
        $16.6$--$17.6\cdot2^h$), proved (forest: 88 to 98\% of the cover attack's $21.45\cdot2^h$;
        \cref{sec:lifetimes}). A layered key is at least 24 high (table below).
  \item[T2, shape.] \emph{Pruned}~\cite{conduition}: one tree of height $h$; key generation builds only a kept subtree
        of $2^b$ leaves and replaces the $h-b$ siblings above it by pseudorandom surrogate nodes. The signer grinds
        the randomizer until the index lands in the kept subtree, $2^{h-b}$ attempts on average ($b=h$ is the full
        key). \emph{Layered}: a hypertree of three layers (table below); key generation builds the top layer only,
        the signer builds the lower trees on the fly and does not grind. At $2^{24}$ and $2^{26}$ signatures its
        24-high tree uses each FTS instance less often, so it takes a lighter FTS.
  \item[T3, hash.] Keccak-256, BLAKE2s or SHA-256.
  \item[T4, FTS.] The \emph{two-level WOTS forest} of \cref{sec:twolevel}, the candidate (the figures below are
        for it unless a table also gives FORS), of 8 trees, or \emph{FORS}, 24 trees of height 10; the
        layered keys of $2^{24}$ and $2^{26}$ take a forest of 6 and 7 trees, and FORS of 19 and 23 trees of
        height 9. Both are proved in Lean, each with its own 39 lifetimes. FORS signatures are 33--34\% larger for
        pruned keys (17--22\% layered), and its threshold preprocessing is about half for pruned keys
        (\cref{sec:twolevel}).
\end{description}

\paragraph{Hash inputs.} $\hash$ is truncated to 16 bytes except where noted. Every input is
$P \concat \mathit{address} \concat \mathit{data}$, with $P$ the 16-byte public parameter and a 16-byte address:
two fields of 4 and 3 bytes, one byte for the call type and chain step, one byte for the layer (from the bottom),
4 bytes for the tree in its layer, 3 zero bytes (layer and tree are zero with one layer). Block cost of $\ell$ bytes:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Keccak-256} (Ethereum's \texttt{keccak256}): $P$ inline; $\lfloor\ell/136\rfloor+1$ Keccak-f[1600]
        permutations.
  \item \textbf{BLAKE2s}: $P$ inline; $\lceil\ell/64\rceil$ compressions (no padding byte), so a tree node
        $P\concat\mathit{address}\concat\mathit{left}\concat\mathit{right}$ is one block.
  \item \textbf{SHA-256}: $P$ alone in a zero-padded first block, compressed once per key and cached; the $\ell$ bytes
        after it cost $\lceil(\ell+9)/64\rceil$ compressions.
\end{itemize}
With all three, a chain step, a tree node, a grinding attempt and a WOTS$^+$C counter try each cost one compression
(the randomizer and the counter sit in the last block of their input). Also:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the digest hashes $m\concat R$. A pruned signer tries $R = R_0 + i\Delta$ for $i = 0, 1, \dots$ (modulo
        $2^{128}$), with $R_0$ and $\Delta$ the two halves of one hash of the secret key and $m$, the lowest bit of
        $\Delta$ set, and keeps the first one that lands in its subtree. $\Delta$ is secret, so a signature does not
        reveal the rejected randomizers
  \item one 32-byte hash of the secret key gives two chain starts
  \item the digest layout is in \cref{sec:twolevel}
\end{itemize}

permutations for Keccak-256), for each FTS; public and secret keys are 32 bytes each. A pruned key has the size and
verification cost of the full key of its height. Throughout, costs are rounded up and lifetimes down.

\section{Lifetimes}
\label{sec:lifetimes}

Proved in Lean~\cite{proofs}, in the random-oracle model, for any hash (SHA-256's padded layout is an injective
re-encoding, argued in the proofs' documentation): if the whole game makes $q$ hash
queries in total (the adversary's, and those of key generation, signing and verification), a forgery has
probability at most $q\cdot2^{-127}$, up to the
signature limits below. One proof, for any height, shape, kept subtree height and forest size, gives the 39 limits, each with its
certificate checked by the Lean kernel; the size and the three verification costs of each of the 10 shapes are
separate theorems; FORS has the same statement and axioms in a second library, with its own 39 limits. Each block of
the table gives the limits, then their ratio to that FTS's cover attack (\cref{sec:twolevel}). The layered key of
each lifetime is the one of \cref{sec:scheme}: at $2^{24}$ and $2^{26}$, 24 high with the lighter FTS. The proof bounds
small query budgets and large ones by two routes; for the FORS key of $2^{24}$ (19 trees) a third route covers the
budgets between them: the large route's survival weight with the small route's accounting, where a correct guess of
a hidden value only wins together with a second event.

\subsection{Threshold signing}
\label{sec:threshold}

\begin{itemize}
  \item $t$-of-$n$ with $n=2t-1$ (2-of-3, 3-of-5, \dots), malicious security (with abort) against $t-1$ operators
  \item distributed key generation (DKG), no trusted dealer
  \item key generation and preprocessing are honest-majority MPCs, so they need all $2t-1$ operators online; signing
        needs only $t$. An arbitrary number of signatures can be preprocessed in advance
  \item any configuration with a small kept subtree or top layer (not the full key, whose $2^h$ leaves would all go
        through the MPC); the MPC never builds the few-time signatures of the whole key
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Idea (adapted from PRAWNS~\cite{PRAWNS} and Riva Labs~\cite{riva}).} Every secret comes from a threshold
PRF $F$: $t$ operators evaluate it together, $t-1$ learn nothing. A signature reveals $x_{i,4-d_i}$, a chain secret
hashed $4-d_i$ times ($d_i$ a digit of its codeword, \cref{sec:twolevel}), which for $d_i<4$ is not an output of $F$, so the MPC publishes the chain values masked under $F$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item position 0, the secret: $F(\mathit{addr})$
  \item positions $j = 1$ to 3 (1 to 2 for WOTS$^+$C): $x_{i,j} \oplus F(\mathit{addr}_{i,j})$, at a fresh address
        $\mathit{addr}_{i,j}$
  \item position 4 (3 for WOTS$^+$C), the top: published
\end{itemize}
Revealing $x_{i,j}$ is then one context-aware evaluation of $F$ at an address the message selects; the rest is public.

\paragraph{Key generation.} The MPC builds only the top layer of a layered key, or the kept subtree and its
surrogates of a pruned key.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item coin tossing: $P$ (and the surrogates of a pruned key)
  \item the key of $F$ (hash-based: each seed by commit-reveal among its holders, the commitments broadcast, else a
        holder could give different seeds to the others, which only aborts signings; lattice-based: a verifiable
        Shamir sharing of $\mathit{msk}$), and the pairwise keys $k_{ij}$
  \item MPC: the WOTS$^+$C chains of every leaf; open the chain ends and build the tree in the clear; publish the
        other chain values masked under $F$, so that a WOTS$^+$C signature is later a few openings of $F$
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Preprocessing (before the message).}
\begin{itemize}
  \item pick an instance (index) uniformly at random (in the kept subtree of a pruned key), from randomness generated
        inside the MPC, so that no coalition can bias or predict it
  \item the MPC generates its forest (and the WOTS$^+$C chains of the lower layers of a layered key) and publishes the
        masked chain values; in the clear, it builds the forest key, the lower trees and the authentication path, and
        opens the WOTS$^+$C signatures that link the instance to the top layer (public anyway once it signs, since
        each WOTS$^+$C key only ever signs one root)
  \item it draws $s=F(\mathit{addr})$ at a fresh address and publishes only $\hash(s)$, a commitment to the signing
        randomness
  \item each instance is prepared for one quorum $J$ of $t$ operators, and only $J$ opens it: an honest member of $J$
        sees every use and refuses a second one (else a corrupt operator could use it with two quorums and, knowing
        $s$ after the first, choose the second message)
  \item a signature consumes its instance, replaced by a fresh one: one instance per signature
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Signing (the quorum $J$ of the instance).}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item agree on $m$
  \item open $s$ and check it against $\hash(s)$; $R_0$ and $\Delta$ are the two halves of $\hash(s, m)$, the lowest
        bit of $\Delta$ set. The commitment, fixed before $m$, stops a signer holding a term of $F$ from shifting $R$
        and so choosing the chains opened
  \item grind in the clear: the first $R = R_0 + i\Delta$ whose index is the prepared instance, about $2^h$ attempts
  \item open $F$ at the addresses of the forest chain values the message selects, and only those; check each against
        the published chain
  \item output $R$, the forest opening and the prepared WOTS$^+$C signatures and paths
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Choice of $F$.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item hash-based, for small $\binom{n}{t-1}$: replicated seeds~\cite{CDI05}, one seed $k_T$ per set $T$ of $t-1$
        operators, held by the others; $F(x)=\bigoplus_T\hash(k_T,x)$
  \item lattice-based (LWE), for large $\binom{n}{t-1}$: the threshold PRF of PRAWNS~\cite{PRAWNS}, with
        $\mathit{msk}$ Shamir-shared, $F(x)=\lfloor\langle\hash(x),\mathit{msk}\rangle\rceil_p$; converting shares
        mod $q$ to bits in the MPC costs a few percent more traffic, neglected here
\end{itemize}
\paragraph{Context-awareness~\cite{PRAWNS}.} To reveal $F(x)$ when a quorum $J$ signs $m$, each signer $i\in J$
sends a part, and the parts combine into $F(x)$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item hash-based: each term $\hash(k_T,x)$ is assigned to one signer of $J$ holding $k_T$; signer $i$ sends the XOR
        of its terms and of $\hash(k_{ij},x,m,J)$ for each co-signer $j$, so the masks cancel in pairs
  \item lattice-based: as in PRAWNS, a rounded share $\lfloor\lambda_i\langle\hash(x),\mathit{sk}_i\rangle\rceil_p$
        plus a rounded share of zero at $(x,m,J)$; the sum is $F(x)$ up to $\pm t$ per coordinate, so $F$ is rounded
        once more to a coarser modulus, and the few candidates are checked against the published chain
\end{itemize}
An honest signer opens only the addresses selected by the digest it computed itself, so an aborted signing reveals
at most its own signature. The last
signer can still complete it alone (fairness is impossible against $t-1$ of $t$~\cite{Cle86}), so a signing that
reaches the opening of $s$ (step 2) consumes its instance and counts as a signature; a wrong part fails the check
against the published chain and aborts the signing.

\paragraph{Threshold costs.} Honest-majority MPC with malicious security (with abort) costs about the semi-honest
price: 1 bit per AND gate per operator for 2-of-3~\cite{AFLNO16}, plus sublinear traffic for the malicious
check~\cite{BGIN19,GS20}. Replicated sharing over $\mathrm{GF}(2)$ generalizes to $n=2t-1$ with $(n-1)+t-1$ bits per
AND gate through one collector: 1, 1.2 and 1.29 bits per operator for 2-of-3, 3-of-5 and 4-of-7 (the local work grows
with $\binom{n-1}{t-1}$, fine for small committees). The MPC hashes only chain steps and FORS leaves (the secrets are outputs
of $F$; keys, trees and the forest key are hashed in the clear): key generation is $2^b\cdot64\cdot3$ steps, $b$ the
height of the kept subtree or top layer, and preprocessing $T\cdot256\cdot6\cdot4$ steps for the forest ($49\,152$
with 8 trees) and $k\,2^a$ leaf hashes for FORS ($24\,576$ for $24\times10$; plus the lower trees' chains of a layered key, and $\hash(s)$). AND gates
per compression: $36.3\cdot10^3$ for Keccak-f[1600] (1\,600 per round,
less the public part of the first and most of the truncated last), $22.6\cdot10^3$ for SHA-256 (Bristol circuit),
$14.9\cdot10^3$ for BLAKE2s (10 rounds of 8 functions of 6 additions modulo $2^{32}$, 31 AND gates each). Traffic per
operator at $2^{28}$ signatures ($h=24$), 2-of-3:

(Keccak-256, BLAKE2s, SHA-256). Online, signing
grinds about $2^h$ digests in the clear ($1.68\cdot10^7$ at $h=24$) and opens $5.47\,T$ forest chain values on
average, at most $8T$ (44 and 64 with 8 trees; FORS: its $k$ secrets), one part per signer per value.

\paragraph{Remark.} The Lean proof covers the single deterministic signer. The prepared instances are uniform and each
is used once, so the indices of threshold signatures are distributed as the single signer's, and the lifetime is
meant to be the configuration's.

\paragraph{Alternative: every forest at key generation.} A pruned key has only $2^b$ instances (4\,096 at $b=12$,
each used about 28 times over the lifetime), so key generation can prepare all of them: the MPC computes every
forest's chains, publishes their masked values, and opens the WOTS$^+$C signatures linking them to the kept subtree.
Signing then needs no MPC: the quorum $J$ agrees on $m$, then draws $R_0$ and $\Delta$ as
$\hash(m, J, c, r_1,\dots,r_t)$, each signer committing to $r_i$ before revealing it, with $c$ the session counter of
$J$; it grinds the $2^{h-b}$ attempts in the clear and opens $F$ at the selected addresses. A signer advances $c$ (the
session counts as a signature) when it reveals its own $r_i$, and each quorum has its own counter range, so there are
at most $N$ in all. Traffic per operator at $h=24$, $b=12$, 2-of-3:
0.92~TB once with Keccak-256 (0.38 and 0.57~TB with BLAKE2s and SHA-256), against 26~TB (10.6 and 16.1~TB) of
preprocessing over the lifetime.

\subsection{Hardware wallets}
\label{sec:ledger}

Grinding about $2^h$ randomizers onto a few prepared instances is too slow on a hardware wallet. Following Riva
Labs~\cite{riva}, the device delegates it to the untrusted host, which proves it with a STARK:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item in its idle time, the device prepares $p$ uniformly random instances (from its secure randomness), as in
        \cref{sec:threshold}: 16 at $h\le24$, 64 at $h=26$ and $h=28$, doubled with BLAKE2s or SHA-256 until
        assisted signing is visibly faster than signing alone (up to 256 for a pruned key with $b=12$, 16 to 64 for a layered key;
        \texttt{scripts/scheme.py}); where signing alone beats the 3~s of the STARK below, the device signs alone
  \item to sign $m$, it derives $R_0$ and $\Delta$ in one hash of its secret key and $m$, and sends $m$, $R_0$,
        $\Delta$ and the $p$ indices to the host
  \item the host finds the least $i$ such that the index of $R_0 + i\Delta$ is one of the $p$, $\approx2^h/p$
        digest hashes on average (at most $2^{22}$), and returns $R = R_0 + i\Delta$ with
        a STARK that $i$ is the least
  \item the device verifies the STARK and finishes the signature from the prepared instance
\end{enumerate}
The STARK needs no zero knowledge; $\Delta$ is no longer secret against the host. The host holds the public data of
each instance (forest trees, WOTS$^+$C signatures and paths); the device keeps only its index and a hash of its FTS
key ($2T$ top nodes for the forest, $2k$ for FORS), recomputes from its seed the chain values (FORS: secrets) it opens,
and checks the host's nodes against that hash.
Minimality makes $R$ a function of $(m, \text{secret key})$ and the prepared set, as for the deterministic signer;
this flow is not covered by the Lean proof.

\paragraph{Costs.} At $h=24$ and $26$ the host hashes $\approx2^{20}$ digests and proves them at 500 thousand Keccak-f
permutations per second (750 thousand SHA-256, 1 million BLAKE2s compressions), 2.1~s with Keccak-256 (8.4~s at $h=28$).
The device verifies the STARK: its hashing alone is about $10^4$ compressions (1.8~s with Keccak-256 at 5.8 thousand
per second on a Ledger Nano S+~\cite{hwbench}), and we count the whole verification, optimistically, as 3~s: 5.2~s in all (11.5~s at $h=28$), less where more
instances are prepared. Preparing an instance costs the device $76\cdot10^3$ Keccak-f permutations for a pruned
key with $b=12$ and $164\cdot10^3$ for a layered one at $2^{28}$ ($343\cdot10^3$
at $h=28$), WOTS$^+$C counter searches and the path included; the first $p$ are prepared once (about 63~min for
the layered key at $h=28$).

\section{The few-time signature: a two-level WOTS forest}
\label{sec:twolevel}

The FTS under each leaf is built from small WOTS keys instead of FORS, an idea from Jonas Nick's (Blockstream)
sig.golf submission~\cite{siggolf}.

\paragraph{Against FORS.} The FORS variant (T4) uses FORS~\cite{FIPS205} with no root hash (the WOTS$^+$C key signs
the $2k$ nodes below the roots), $k=24$ trees of height $a=10$ for every pruned key, so that verification does
not depend on the pruning, and for the layered keys from $2^{28}$ on; its digest is $h+240$ bits, from two hash calls.
24 is the fewest trees for which the full key still lasts $2^{h+4}$ signatures: with 23, even the cover attack falls
short at every height. There, its cover lifetime is 83--85\% of the forest's (\texttt{scripts/scheme.py}); its proved
lifetimes (\cref{sec:lifetimes}) are 83--98\% of its cover attack, and 12--16\% below the forest's. The layered keys
of $2^{24}$ and $2^{26}$, 24 high, take 19 and 23 trees of height 9 (a one-call digest), the fewest whose cover
attack reaches $2^{24}$ and $2^{26}$: $2^{24}$ and $7.16\cdot10^7$ proved signatures, 85\% and 87\% of their cover
attack, against $2.66\cdot10^7$ and $1.44\cdot10^8$ for the forest of 6 and 7 trees. With 19 trees the two routes of
the proof leave a gap; a third route closes it (\cref{sec:lifetimes}). At $h=26$, full key,
Keccak-256, in permutations (preprocessing: the hashes of one FTS instance inside the threshold MPC):

FORS halves its preprocessing because only the leaf layer $\hash(\text{secret})$ touches the shared secrets:
once the leaves leave the MPC they are public, and the $k(2^a-2)$ nodes above them are a public computation.
The forest cannot do the same --- its MPC cost is the WOTS chain steps, and each step consumes the previous
step's secret output, so none of the chain can leave the MPC early.

\paragraph{Per key.} Take a key that has made $N$ signatures, $N$ being the cover lifetime of its FTS. Let $Y$ be
the probability, for this key, that a random digest selects an FTS opening its signatures have already revealed. $Y$
changes from key to key (it depends on where the $N$ signatures landed and what they revealed); its average over
keys is $2^{-127}$, by the choice of $N$. The standard deviation of $Y$, divided by $2^{-127}$, at $h=24$
(\href{https://github.com/TomWambsgans/sphincs.art/blob/main/scripts/key_variance.py}{\texttt{scripts/key\_variance.py}}):
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
  \toprule
  & full key & $b=14$ \\
  \midrule
  two-level WOTS forest & 0.079 & 0.98 \\
  FORS, $k=24$, $a=10$ & 0.047 & 0.63 \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\paragraph{WOTS keys.} 6 chains of length 5, with leaf $\hash(\text{6 chain tops})$. A codeword is a vector
$d\in\{0,\dots,4\}^6$ with $\sum_i d_i=5$; there are 246 of them. Signing at $d$ reveals each chain $d_i$ steps below
its top, so verification always takes 5 steps. The fixed sum acts as the checksum: one signature reaches no other
codeword; several signatures by the same key reach exactly the codewords below their componentwise max.

\paragraph{Trees.} The FTS has $T$ independent trees of height 4 (\cref{fig:forest}): $T=8$, but 6 and 7 for the
layered keys of $2^{24}$ and $2^{26}$ (\cref{sec:scheme}). Each of the 16 leaves of a tree
is $\hash(S_0,S_1)$, where $S_j$ is the pair of top nodes of subtree $j$ (height 3, over 8 WOTS keys; no subtree root).
In each tree, the message picks a leaf, and in both of its subtrees a WOTS key and a codeword.

\paragraph{Encoding.} Bit fields and one 256-entry table: the encoding rejects no digest. Each tree takes 26 bits:
4 for the leaf, then, per subtree, 3 for the WOTS key and 8 for a table index. The $2T$ table indices are the
first $2T$ bytes of the digest; the bits after them, little-endian, hold the $2T$ WOTS keys, then the $T$ leaves,
then the instance index (with $T=8$, no field lies across two 64-bit words). With the $h\le28$ bits of the index,
the digest is $h + 26T\le236$ bits: one hash. The table: sort the 246 codewords by decreasing $\sum_i d_i^2$, then in
increasing lexicographic order (first digit most significant); entry $t$ is codeword number $t \bmod 214$. Spread-out
codewords are easier to forge once a key has signed twice, so the 32 most spread-out ones are left out, and the 42
most concentrated ones appear twice.

\paragraph{Costs.} Per tree:
\begin{itemize}
  \item size: 22 values, i.e.\ 6 chain values and 3 nodes per subtree, plus 4 nodes in the tree
  \item verification (Keccak-256): 20 permutations, i.e.\ per subtree 5 chain steps, 1 for the leaf and 2 for the path, then 1 for
        $\hash(S_0,S_1)$ and 3 for the path in the tree
  \item signing: $16\cdot2\cdot8 = 256$ WOTS keys of 28 Keccak-f permutations each
\end{itemize}
Every signature has the same size and verification cost. The WOTS$^+$C key that signs the forest uses a target sum
of 128 instead of 120: 8 fewer chain steps at verification, for about 14\,700 counter tries instead of 830 when
signing ($13.9\cdot10^3$ more permutations, counted in \cref{sec:usage}).

\paragraph{Security.} A forgery at an instance with $r$ prior signatures needs every tree to be covered: in both
subtrees, the target WOTS key must have been opened, and the target codeword must lie below the componentwise max of
those openings. Let $f_t$ be the probability that $t$ random signatures by one WOTS key cover a random table entry
($f_1=0.0052$, $f_2=0.068$, $f_3=0.17$). Then
\[
\mathrm{cover}(r)=\Big(\sum_{u\ge1}\mathrm{Bin}(r,\tfrac1{16})(u)\Big[\sum_{t\ge1}\mathrm{Bin}(u,\tfrac18)(t)\,f_t\Big]^2\Big)^T .
\]
The cover lifetime (the attack figure) is the largest $N$ with $2^{127}\,2^{b-h}\,\mathbb{E}_{r\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(N/2^b)}[\mathrm{cover}(r)]\le1$:
the signatures spread over the $2^b$ instances of the kept subtree, and a forger's digest must also land in it. This
factor $2^{b-h}$ is not credited by hypertree pruning~\cite{conduition}, which cuts the lifetime by the full factor
$2^{h-b}$ although the forger pays the signer's grinding cost per attempt; it is worth about $2^{(h-b)/18}$, from
$+17\%$ at $h=24$, $b=20$ to a factor 1.8 at $b=8$. The best known attack also uses the WOTS$^+$C key (a counter
search for a neighbouring encoding, then one chain preimage); at $h=24$ it is 100, 99, 96, 95, 95, 93 and 91\% of the
forest's cover figure for $b=24,20,14,13,12,10,8$ (a Monte Carlo estimate, \texttt{formal/sphincs/scripts/forest\_attack.py}). For FORS, a tree is covered when the target leaf
is among the revealed ones, so $\mathrm{cover}(r)=(1-(1-2^{-a})^r)^k$, with the same lifetime rule (the loads
exactly binomial). For the forest at $h=24$, rounded down (the last row is \cref{sec:lifetimes}):

  \toprule
  $b$ & 24 & 20 & 14 & 13 & 12 & 10 & 8 \\
  \midrule
  forest, cover & $3.59\cdot10^8$ & $2.63\cdot10^7$ & $5.18\cdot10^5$ & $2.68\cdot10^5$ & $1.39\cdot10^5$ & $3.74\cdot10^4$ & $1.00\cdot10^4$ \\
  \midrule
