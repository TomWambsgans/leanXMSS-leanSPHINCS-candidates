\section{The scheme}
\label{sec:scheme}

One scheme with three toggles, 24 configurations in all. Every configuration has the same shape as
SLH-DSA-128-24 from NIST's SP~800-230~\cite{SP800230}, with the differences:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a \emph{two-level WOTS forest} (\cref{sec:twolevel}; credit: Jonas Nick, sig.golf~\cite{siggolf}) instead of FORS as the few-time signature (FTS):
        signatures are 25\% smaller and verification 25\% cheaper, at the same signing cost and longer lifetimes
  \item WOTS$^+$C~\cite{HK22C} instead of WOTS$^+$: 64 chains of length $w=4$, with a target sum of 123
  \item the WOTS$^+$C encoding absorbs the FTS: it hashes the two nodes below the root of each of the 8 trees of
        the forest, then the counter; there is no FTS public key and no tree of the forest has a root hash
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The toggles.}
\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item[T1, lifetime.] The height $h\in\{22,24,26,28\}$ of the index, for lifetimes of about $2^{26}$, $2^{28}$,
        $2^{30}$ and $2^{32}$ signatures: the full key lasts $N\approx21.45\cdot2^h$ signatures (\cref{sec:lifetimes}).
  \item[T2, shape.] \emph{Pruned}: one tree of height $h$. Key generation builds only a kept subtree of $2^b$ leaves
        and replaces the $h-b$ siblings above it by pseudorandom surrogate nodes~\cite{conduition}; the signer
        grinds the randomizer until the index lands in the kept subtree, $2^{h-b}$ attempts on average
        ($b=h$ is the full key). The forger must land there too, so the lifetime is not cut by the whole pruning
        factor $2^{h-b}$ (\cref{sec:lifetimes}). \emph{Layered}: a hypertree of three layers, a top layer of at most 12
        over two equal layers of at least 6; key generation builds the top layer only, the
        signer builds the trees below it on the fly and does not grind, and the lifetime is the full key's.
  \item[T3, hash.] Keccak-256, BLAKE2s or SHA-256, each with its own layout (below).
\end{description}

The two lower layers add two WOTS$^+$C signatures with their counters, 2\,056 bytes, and 156 Keccak-f permutations
of verification (174 compressions with BLAKE2s or SHA-256).

\paragraph{Hash inputs.} $\hash$ is truncated to 16 bytes except where noted. Every hash input is
$P \concat \mathit{address} \concat \mathit{data}$: the public parameter $P$ (16 bytes), then a 16-byte address:
two fields of 4 and 3 bytes, one byte for the type of the call and the step in a chain, one byte for the layer
(counted from the bottom), 4 bytes for the tree in its layer, and 3 zero bytes. With one layer the layer and
tree bytes are zero. The three hashes differ in how they cut an input into blocks:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Keccak-256} (Ethereum's \texttt{keccak256}): $P$ inline; $\ell$ bytes cost $\lfloor\ell/136\rfloor+1$
        Keccak-f[1600] permutations (the padding takes at least one byte). The 80-byte digest input is one block.
  \item \textbf{BLAKE2s}: $P$ inline; $\ell$ bytes cost $\lceil\ell/64\rceil$ compressions (the last block is
        zero-padded, with no padding byte), so a tree node, $P\concat\mathit{address}\concat\mathit{left}\concat\mathit{right}$,
        is exactly one block.
  \item \textbf{SHA-256}: $P$ alone in a zero-padded first block of 64 bytes, compressed once per key and cached, so a
        chain step and a node cost one compression; the $\ell$ bytes after it cost $\lceil(\ell+9)/64\rceil$ compressions,
        with the at least 9 bytes of padding.
\end{itemize}
With all three, a grinding attempt costs one compression (the randomizer $R$ is alone in the last block of the
digest input; SHA-256 puts 16 zero bytes before it), and so does a WOTS$^+$C counter (the counter is in the last block of
the encoding input). Other details:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the digest hashes $m\concat R$. A pruned signer tries $R = R_0 + i\Delta$ for $i = 0, 1, \dots$ (modulo
        $2^{128}$), with $R_0$ and $\Delta$ the two halves of one hash of the secret key and $m$, the lowest bit of
        $\Delta$ set, and keeps the first one that lands in its subtree. $\Delta$ is secret: a signature does not tell which
        randomizers were tried and rejected before it
  \item the secrets are derived from the secret key two at a time: one hash gives 32 bytes, i.e.\ two chain starts
  \item the digest is one call of $h + 8\cdot26$ bits: the 16 codeword indices are its first 16 bytes, then the 16
        WOTS keys (3 bits each), the 8 leaves (4 bits each) and the index ($h$ bits), in that order, so that no field
        lies across two 64-bit words
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The 24 configurations.} Size of a signature and compressions of a verification (Keccak-f[1600]
permutations for Keccak-256); the public key and the secret key are 32 bytes each. A pruned key has the size and
the verification cost of the full key of its height.

\section{Lifetimes}
\label{sec:lifetimes}

Proved in Lean~\cite{proofs}, in the random-oracle model: an adversary making $q$ hash queries (key generation,
signing and verification included) forges with probability at most $q\cdot2^{-127}$, up to the signature limits
below, whatever the hash. The proof does not depend on the hash: one proof, for any height, shape and kept subtree
height, gives the 32 limits below, each with its certificate checked by the Lean kernel; the size and the three
verification costs (one per hash) of each of the 8 shapes are separate theorems. The second half of the table is the ratio to the lifetime of the best
attack in the cover model (\cref{sec:twolevel}).

\subsection{Threshold signing}
\label{sec:threshold}

\begin{itemize}
  \item $t$-of-$n$, malicious security (with abort) against fewer than $\min(t, n/2)$ corrupt operators: key
        generation and preprocessing are MPCs among the $n$ operators with an honest majority
  \item key generation: distributed (DKG), no trusted dealer
  \item any configuration; the MPC never builds the few-time signatures of the whole key
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Idea (adapted from PRAWNS~\cite{PRAWNS}).} Every secret is derived from a threshold PRF $F$: $t$ operators
evaluate it together, $t-1$ learn nothing (context-aware, see below). A signature reveals $x_{i,4-d_i}$, the chain secret
hashed $4-d_i$ times, which no threshold PRF outputs, so the MPC publishes the chain values encrypted under $F$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item position 0, the secret: $F(\mathit{addr})$
  \item positions $j = 1$ to 3: computed by the MPC, published as $x_{i,j} \oplus F(\mathit{addr}_{i,j})$, for a
        fresh address $\mathit{addr}_{i,j}$
  \item position 4, the top: published
\end{itemize}
Revealing $x_{i,j}$ is then one context-aware evaluation of $F$: the $t$ signers evaluate $F$ at the addresses the message
selects, and everything else is read from public data.

\paragraph{Key generation.} The MPC builds the XMSS part alone: the top layer of a layered key, or the kept subtree
and its surrogates of a pruned key. It never builds a few-time signature.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item coin tossing: $P$ (and the surrogates of a pruned key)
  \item the key of $F$ (hash-based: each seed by commit-reveal among its holders; lattice-based: a verifiable
        Shamir sharing of $\mathit{msk}$), and the pairwise keys $k_{ij}$
  \item MPC: the WOTS$^+$C chains of every leaf of the top layer (or of the kept subtree); open the chain ends, then build the
        tree in the clear
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Preprocessing (before the message).} Pick 16 instances (indices) uniformly at random, in the kept subtree of
a pruned key, from randomness the operators generate securely together (inside the MPC), so that no coalition can bias
or predict it. For each, the MPC generates its forest, publishes the masked chain values as above, and builds in the clear its
forest key, the WOTS$^+$C signatures that link it to the top layer (with the trees of the lower layers of a layered key)
and the authentication paths. A signature consumes one prepared instance, which is then replaced by a fresh one drawn the same way,
so the preprocessing is one instance per signature.

\paragraph{Signing (any $t$).}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item agree on $m$
  \item reveal $F(\mathit{rand}, m)$ (as below); $R_0$ and $\Delta$ are the two halves of
        $\hash(F(\mathit{rand}, m), m)$, the lowest bit of $\Delta$ set. $R$ depends only on $m$ and the prepared set, so
        nobody can bias it, and a retry of $m$ opens the same values
  \item grind in the clear: the first $R = R_0 + i\Delta$ whose index is one of the 16 prepared instances, about
        $2^h/16$ attempts
  \item open $F$ at the addresses of the forest chain values the message selects, and only those; check each against
        the published chain
  \item output $R$, the forest opening and the prepared WOTS$^+$C signatures and paths
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Choice of $F$.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item hash-based, for small $\binom{n}{t-1}$: replicated seeds~\cite{CDI05}, one seed $k_T$ per set $T$ of $t-1$
        operators, held by the others; $F(x)=\bigoplus_T\hash(k_T,x)$. Hash only
  \item lattice-based, for large $\binom{n}{t-1}$: the threshold PRF of PRAWNS~\cite{PRAWNS},
        $F(x)=\lfloor\langle\hash(x),\mathit{msk}\rangle\rceil_p$, $\mathit{msk}$ Shamir-shared. LWE
\end{itemize}
\paragraph{Context-awareness~\cite{PRAWNS}.} To reveal $F(x)$ when a quorum $J$ signs $m$, each signer
$i\in J$ sends a part, and the parts combine into $F(x)$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item hash-based: each term $\hash(k_T,x)$ is assigned to one signer of $J$ holding $k_T$. Signer $i$ sends
        the XOR of its terms and of $\hash(k_{ij},x,m,J)$ for each co-signer $j$, with $k_{ij}$ a key shared by
        $i$ and $j$: each mask is sent twice and cancels, so the parts XOR to $F(x)$
  \item lattice-based: as in PRAWNS, a rounded share $\lfloor\lambda_i\langle\hash(x),\mathit{sk}_i\rangle\rceil_p$
        plus a rounded share of zero evaluated at $(x,m,J)$; the sum is $F(x)$ up to $\pm t$, and the
        candidates are checked against the published chain
\end{itemize}
Parts from signings of different $(m,J)$ don't combine, so an aborted signing reveals at most its own signature. The last
signer to send can still complete it alone (fairness is impossible against $t-1$ of $t$~\cite{Cle86}), so a signing that
reaches step 4 consumes its instance and counts as a signature; a missing or wrong part is checked against the
published chain, which identifies its sender.


\paragraph{Threshold costs.} Honest-majority MPC with malicious security (with abort) costs essentially the
semi-honest price: about 1 bit per AND gate per operator with three operators~\cite{AFLNO16}, the malicious check
adding sublinear traffic~\cite{BGIN19,GS20}. A compression is
$36.3\cdot10^3$ AND gates for Keccak-f[1600] (1\,600 per round; part of the first round is public, and the truncated
output skips most of the last one), $22.6\cdot10^3$ for SHA-256 (the Bristol circuit) and $14.9\cdot10^3$ for BLAKE2s
(10 rounds of 8 functions of 6 additions modulo $2^{32}$, 31 AND gates each). Traffic per operator at $h=24$, at 1 bit per AND gate:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
  \toprule
  & Keccak-256 & BLAKE2s & SHA-256 \\
  \midrule
  keygen, layered or pruned $b=12$ & 4.3~GB & 1.8~GB & 2.8~GB \\
  preprocessing, pruned (one instance) & 269~MB & 114~MB & 173~MB \\
  preprocessing, layered (and two lower trees) & 406~MB & 172~MB & 262~MB \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Online, signing grinds about $2^h/16$ digests in the clear ($1.05\cdot10^6$ at $h=24$) and opens 44 forest chain values
on average, at most 64: each signer sends one part per value.

\paragraph{Remarks.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The Lean proof covers the single deterministic signer. The prepared instances are uniform and each is used
        once, so the indices of the threshold signatures are distributed as the single signer's, and the lifetime
        is meant to be the one of the configuration.
  \item The key shares can be refreshed, and the committee changed (including $n$ and $t$), with the same
        public key: hash-based, the new committee generates fresh seeds, defining a new PRF $F'$, and an MPC
        among $t$ old and the new operators publishes $c(x)=F(x)\oplus F'(x)$ for every published address $x$, so
        that the secret at $x$ is $F'(x)\oplus c(x)$; lattice-based, $t$ old operators reshare $\mathit{msk}$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Hardware wallets}
\label{sec:ledger}

A hardware wallet keeps the secret key and signs, but grinding $2^h/p$ randomizers is too slow there. The idea of
delegating that work to the untrusted host and proving it with a STARK comes from Riva~\cite{riva}.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item the device prepares $p=\max(16, 2^{h-20})$ uniformly random instances (from its secure randomness) in advance, as in \cref{sec:threshold} (in its
        idle time), so that the host grinds $\approx2^{20}$ digests or fewer: 16 at $h\le24$, 64 at $h=26$, 256 at $h=28$
  \item to sign $m$, it derives $R_0$ and $\Delta$ in one hash of its secret key and $m$, and sends $m$, $R_0$,
        $\Delta$ and the $p$ indices to the host
  \item the host finds the least $i$ such that the index of $R_0 + i\Delta$ is one of the $p$, $\approx2^h/p\le2^{20}$ on average
        digest hashes, and returns $R = R_0 + i\Delta$ with a STARK that $i$ is the least
  \item the device verifies the STARK and finishes the signature from the prepared instance
\end{enumerate}
The statement of the STARK is public, so it needs no zero knowledge; $\Delta$ is no longer secret against the host.
The WOTS$^+$C signatures and paths of an instance are public once it signs (each WOTS$^+$C key only ever signs one
root), so the host can hold them for the device, which keeps a hash of each.
The minimality makes $R$ a function of $(m, \text{secret key})$ and the prepared set, as for the deterministic signer; this flow is not covered by the Lean proof.

\paragraph{Hardware wallet costs.} At $h\ge24$ the host hashes $\approx2^{20}$ digests on average and proves them in a STARK at about
$2^{19}$ hashes per second, 2~s. The device verifies the STARK in about $10^4$ compressions, which we count as 3~s on a Ledger Nano S+
(1.7~s with Keccak-256 at 5.8 thousand compressions per second~\cite{hwbench}): about 5~s in all. Preparing an instance costs the
device $59\cdot10^3$ Keccak-f permutations for a pruned key and $89\cdot10^3$ for a layered one at $h=24$
($179\cdot10^3$ at $h=28$), in its idle time; a signature consumes one instance, and the first $p$ are prepared once
(about 2.2~h for the layered key at $h=28$).

\section{The few-time signature: a two-level WOTS forest}
\label{sec:twolevel}

The few-time signature (FTS) under each tree leaf is built from small WOTS keys instead of FORS. Against
FORS with $k=24$ trees of height $a=10$, at the same parameters otherwise ($h=24$), signatures are 25\% smaller and
verification 25\% cheaper, for about the same signing cost and longer lifetimes, proved in Lean~\cite{proofs}.
The idea is inspired by Jonas Nick's (Blockstream) sig.golf submission~\cite{siggolf}.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
  \toprule
  FTS & size & verification & FTS signing \\
  \midrule
  FORS, $k=24$, $a=10$ & 5\,652 bytes & 352 permutations & $61\cdot10^3$ permutations \\
  two-level WOTS forest & 4\,244 bytes & 265 permutations & $59\cdot10^3$ permutations \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\paragraph{WOTS keys.} The FTS is built from small WOTS keys: 6 chains of length 5, with leaf
$\hash(\text{6 chain tops})$. A codeword is a vector $d\in\{0,\dots,4\}^6$ with $\sum_i d_i=5$; there are 246 of them.
Signing at $d$ reveals each chain $d_i$ steps below its top. The verifier hashes up to the tops: always 5
steps in total. The fixed sum acts as the checksum: from one signature, hashing forward cannot reach any other codeword. From
several signatures by the same key, it reaches exactly the codewords below their componentwise max.

\paragraph{Trees.} The FTS has 8 independent trees of height 4, and the WOTS$^+$C encoding hashes their 8 roots. Each of
the 16 leaves of a tree is $\hash(R_0,R_1)$, where $R_0$ and $R_1$ are the roots of two subtrees of height 3 over 8
WOTS keys each. In each tree, the message picks a leaf, and in both of its subtrees a WOTS key and a codeword. A tree or a subtree has
no root hash: in $\hash(R_0,R_1)$ and in the encoding, $R$ stands for the two nodes just below the root.

\paragraph{Encoding.} Bit fields and one 256-entry table: the encoding rejects no digest. Each tree takes 26 bits:
4 for the leaf, then, per subtree, 3 for the WOTS key and 8 for a table index. The 16 table indices are the first
16 bytes of the digest; its last 16 bytes, as a little-endian integer, hold the 16 WOTS keys, then the 8 leaves,
then the instance index, so no field lies across two 64-bit words. The table has 256 entries: sort the 246
codewords by decreasing $\sum_i d_i^2$, then in increasing lexicographic order (first digit most significant); entry $t$ is codeword number $t \bmod 214$.
Spread-out codewords are easier to forge once a key has signed twice, so the 32 most spread-out ones are left out,
and the 42 most concentrated ones appear twice. With the $h\le28$ bits of the instance index, the digest is at
most 236 bits, so it still takes one hash.

\paragraph{Costs.} Per tree:
\begin{itemize}
  \item size: 22 values, i.e.\ 6 chain values and 3 nodes per subtree, plus 4 nodes in the tree
  \item verification: 20 permutations, i.e.\ per subtree 5 chain steps, 1 for the leaf and 2 for the path, then 1 for
        $\hash(R_0,R_1)$ and 3 for the path in the tree
  \item signing: $16\cdot2\cdot8 = 256$ WOTS keys of 28 permutations each
\end{itemize}
Every signature has the same size and verification cost, and keygen is unchanged. The WOTS$^+$C key that signs
the forest uses a target sum of 123 instead of the 120 that would suit FORS: 3 fewer chain steps at verification,
for about 2\,200 counter tries instead of 830 when signing ($1.4\cdot10^3$ more permutations, still within the
signing costs of \cref{sec:scheme}).

\paragraph{Security.} A forgery at an instance with $r$ prior signatures needs every tree to be covered: in
both subtrees, the target WOTS key must have been opened, and the target codeword must lie below the componentwise
max of those openings. Let $f_t$ be the probability that $t$ random signatures by one WOTS key cover a random codeword
($f_1=0.0052$, $f_2=0.068$, $f_3=0.17$). Then
\[
\mathrm{cover}(r)=\Big(\sum_{u\ge1}\mathrm{Bin}(r,\tfrac1{16})(u)\Big[\sum_{t\ge1}\mathrm{Bin}(u,\tfrac18)(t)\,f_t\Big]^2\Big)^8 .
\]
The lifetime is the largest $N$ with $2^{127}\,2^{b-h}\,\mathbb{E}_{r\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(N/2^b)}[\mathrm{cover}(r)]\le1$:
the signatures spread over the $2^b$ instances of the kept subtree, and a forger's digest must also land in it.
The factor $2^{b-h}$ is what hypertree pruning~\cite{conduition} does not charge the forger: its rule reduces
the lifetime by the full pruning factor $2^{h-b}$, which is the signer's grinding cost, but the forger pays that
same cost per attempt. Here the credit is worth about $2^{(h-b)/18}$, from $+17\%$ at $h=24$, $b=20$ to a factor 1.8 at $b=8$.
At $h=24$, the same cover rule applied to FORS gives the first row, and the two-level forest is about 19\%
ahead at every subtree height. The best known attack also uses the WOTS$^+$C key (a counter search for a neighbouring
encoding, then one chain preimage): for the forest it is the cover figure, except at $b=8$ where it is 2\% lower~\cite{proofs}. Every figure is rounded down; the last row repeats the proved lifetimes of \cref{sec:lifetimes}.
